## 3 · The water

The network says whose truck, from where, and whether the sensor can be believed. The sensor says how much. This chapter is the sensor: what it measures, how litres are derived, which checks run on the trace, and what is simulated in Phase 1.

### What the sensor is

A level-and-turbidity probe on the payer's own tank, with its own SIM. In Phase 3 that is an LTE-M device costing roughly $15–30 plus a ~$10 turbidity probe, installed once per tank on the payer's site. One sensor covers every contractor who ever delivers to that tank, which is what makes the unit economics improve with volume (chapter 7).

In Phase 1 there is no hardware. The trace is generated by `saqqa/sensor/simulator.py` and the dashboard says "simulated sensor" in the panel title. Do not let anyone leave the room thinking otherwise.

### How litres are derived

The demo tank (K7) is a 20 m³ tank, 200 cm tall, so one centimetre of level is 100 litres (`config.TANKS`). The delivered volume is the level rise during the rising window, times litres per centimetre:

- level at 08:30: 38 cm
- rising window: found as the span where the 3-minute slope exceeds 2 cm (the noise floor is ±0.35 cm per sample)
- rise in the honest trip: about 97 cm, so about 9.7 m³ against a claim of 10

The agent pays for measured litres, never claimed ones. A gap under 10% between measured and claimed is treated as normal metering variance, not a shortfall; that rule is written into the audit-note prompt as well as the gate, because the model once described a 3% gap as "falling short".

### Why the trace is a curve and not a line

Water leaving a tanker through a hose does not arrive at a constant rate. The model ramps discharge from zero to about 8 litres per second over roughly two minutes, holds, and tapers, which draws an S-curve in level over about 22 minutes for a 10 m³ load. The shape matters because two of the plausibility checks look at shape, not just at the total:

| Check | What it looks for | What it catches |
|---|---|---|
| Step artefact | more than 25 cm arriving in a single one-minute sample | a spoofed or replayed feed; a pump cannot do that |
| Pump-time plausibility | dwell shorter than 60% of the minutes needed to move the measured litres at 8 L/s | a trace that claims more water than the truck could have discharged in the time it was there |
| Rise-versus-dwell overlap | the minutes the level rose must sit inside the operator's dwell window | a tank that rose before the truck arrived (the run to watch, chapter 5) |
| Flat trace during dwell | dwell of 15 minutes or more with a rise under 0.5 m³ | showed up, never opened the valve |
| Turbidity during the fill | above 5 NTU while the level rises | untreated water in a treated-water contract; the substitution attack |
| Tank room | measured rise close to the space that was left in the tank | a "short fill" that was the tank being full, so dispatch is flagged, not the contractor |
| Capacity accounting | litres credited to a truck since its last verified source visit, plus this delivery, must not exceed the truck | trip inflation: a fourth delivery off one load |

The values (25 cm, 60%, 8 L/s, 15 min, 0.5 m³, 5 NTU) are in `saqqa/agent/gate.py` and `saqqa/config.py`. They are engineering defaults chosen to be defensible, not tuned on data; say so if asked.

### Turbidity, and why a $10 probe is on the tank at all

The best attack anyone found against Saqqa in the red-team round is substitution inside the zone: the real truck sells its clean load at a farm 1.5 km away while an accomplice dumps dirty well water into the tank. The level rises, the truck is "in zone", the network is satisfied. The answer is not a network answer. It is the turbidity probe: dirty water reads tens of NTU against a treated-water floor of 5, the fill is flagged, payment held. UNHCR's own form already logs turbidity and chlorine per trip, because substitution is a known fraud. After the probe, the attack costs a second vehicle, a second load and a second driver to pocket the clean/dirty price spread, which is about what honest delivery costs.

We never claim the network stops substitution. We claim the network still attests that 10 m³ of contracted water left the well and did not reach a clean tank, which turns a quality alarm into a diversion case.

### The order of evidence

The trace is ours. That is the sensor's weakness as a witness: it is the payer's hardware, on the payer's site, and a payer's employee or a contractor's accomplice could in principle manipulate it. So the agent reads evidence in a fixed order:

1. the operator's network first, because its timestamps are external and belong to nobody in the transaction;
2. our sensor second, corroborated or rejected against those timestamps;
3. the payee's number last, before any money moves.

When the two disagree, the network wins. In the trip called "the agent doubts its own sensor", the tank rises 9.8 m³ between 09:05 and 09:27 and the operator's stamps put the truck in the zone from 09:35; the measurement is not believed and nothing is credited. A meter that can be overruled by a witness the meter's owner does not control is the design.

### What "unverified" means, and why it is never fraud

An unreachable sensor makes the trip unverified. The gate returns HOLD, raises a maintenance ticket, and sends the trip back to the paper process it came from. Nobody is accused, nothing is deducted. This is deliberate and it is a commercial position, not only an ethical one: a system that penalised contractors for the payer's own hardware failing would not survive its first month in the field. The honest hauler's experience of Saqqa is same-day settlement and a record the operator stands behind; the only people it bites are the ones who were stealing.

### Phase 3, briefly

The interface between the simulator and the agent is one function that returns 1-minute samples of level and turbidity for a window. A real probe replaces that function and nothing else changes. What changes in the field is everything around it: installation, calibration against the tank's geometry, power, a reachability baseline per site, and the operating rule that a sensor offline for more than a day is a maintenance visit, not a data problem.
